CS 247G · Design for Play

Myodyssey

An educational point-and-click adventure game about habitat loss and survival, told from the perspective of a woodland lemming.

Play on itch.io ↗ View GitHub Repo ↗ Soundtrack Playtest Videos
My Role Game Concept, Level 1 Dev, UI Integration & Soundtrack
Timeline Spring 2026 (10 Weeks)
Team & Testing 5-Person Team · 9 Playtests Across 4 Versions
Built With HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Figma

01 · The Concept

How the Game Works

Myodyssey grew out of our team name, myopus—a small woodland lemming. We wanted to build a game centered on nature, vulnerability, and survival, where players experience what it feels like to be a small animal navigating a forest reshaped by human development.

Choosing the Right Format: Early on, we considered making a 1990s-style graphical point-and-click game, but realized that collaborating across a mix of Windows and Mac laptops would make development and testing difficult. Instead, I developed our core concept as a browser-based, text-and-illustration adventure game in HTML and JavaScript. That choice let our team build smoothly together and kept the game approachable for people who don't usually play video games.

Telling the Story Through Puzzles: Rather than separating the story from the gameplay, we built each chapter around items and puzzles that move the journey forward. In Chapter 1, players click highlighted words in the story text to gather ingredients from the forest, cook a vegetable stew, and trade it with a beaver blocking the path. In Chapters 2 and 3, players complete minigames to keep moving deeper into the woods as the environment shifts from a safe, cozy storybook forest into a darker, polluted landscape.

02 · My Contribution

What I Built

On our five-person team, I helped shape the initial game concept, organize roles and tasks, and bring everyone's work together into one cohesive game:

Building Chapter 1: I designed and coded the opening stage of the game, including the interactive text mechanics, the inventory system, and the vegetable stew puzzle.

Combining Levels & Unifying the UI: Because teammates built different chapters in parallel, our early versions looked and felt disconnected from level to level. I combined the individual chapters into a single playable package and updated the UI across the game so the text boxes, fonts, and storybook art style felt consistent from start to finish.

Composing the Soundtrack: To help set the mood as the forest changes around the player, I composed, arranged, and mixed the game's original five-track soundtrack.

03 · Playtesting

Testing & Iteration

Between Weeks 7 and 10, we ran 9 playtests across 4 versions of the game, testing with everyone from non-gamers and UX designers to experienced gamers. Each round showed us where players were getting stuck or losing the thread of the story:

Making Interactions Clear (v1 → v2): Our first testers had trouble remembering which passages they had already read and didn't always realize which words were clickable. We updated the text so already-read passages grayed out, added small icons to areas with interactive items, and introduced an on-screen inventory so players could easily see what they had collected.

Fixing Bugs & Hardware Quirks (v2 → v4): In later playtests, we noticed that our minigame speeds were accidentally tied to each computer's screen refresh rate—making some puzzles way too fast on newer monitors. We also found that long dialogue sections could trap players who didn't have a mouse scroll wheel. We fixed the minigame timing so it ran at the same speed on every computer, added automatic scrolling with extra padding for long text, and clarified the instructions on our trickiest puzzles.

Designing for Accessibility: Working alongside a colorblind teammate, we made sure no puzzle relied on color alone to solve. After a playtester flagged flashing effects in a Chapter 3 minigame, we also toned down the flashes and slowed them down so the game would be safe for photosensitive players.

Knowing Which Feedback to Keep

During mid-stage testing, a few testers who mentioned they didn't feel strongly about animals found the game's ending abrupt and sad. However, testers in our target audience—people who care about animals and enjoy story-driven games—told us the ending was the most impactful part of the experience. Instead of softening the ending to please everyone, we kept the story intact and focused on fixing the usability bugs that got in the way of experiencing it.
